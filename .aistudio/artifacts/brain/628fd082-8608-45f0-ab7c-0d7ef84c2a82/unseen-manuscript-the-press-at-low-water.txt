The Albion hand-press in the back room of Number 14 Wharf Lane had not printed a broadside since the winter of seventy-eight, yet it still smelled of linseed oil, turpentine, and the stubborn river damp that crept up through the flagstones whenever the Trent ran high. Miriam Locke stood on the threshold with her umbrella dripping onto the doormat, listening to the gas meter in the hall give its small, metallic hiccup every forty seconds.

She was sixty-four, and her knees had begun to predict rain with greater accuracy than the BBC shipping forecast. Behind her, three tea-chests of unbound botanical plates sat in the back of a hired van while the driver waited with his engine running and his meter ticking like a tiny debt collector.

“Aunt Miriam?”

Jude came out from behind the tall oak type-cases, wiping his ink-stained knuckles on a coarse canvas apron he had tied twice around his narrow waist. At nineteen he had his father’s long, apologetic shoulders and the habit of standing slightly sideways in doorways, as though he expected someone more important to come through at any moment.

“Don’t wipe your hands on that apron, Jude,” Miriam said, setting her wet umbrella in the cast-iron stand by the door. “Your father used that cloth for lifting hot lead off the galley. If you get turps on the hem, it’ll smell till Christmas.”

“There’s no lead on the galley, Aunt Miriam. Only the pearwood blocks you sent down from Norwich last Tuesday.” He looked past her toward the street. “You’re early. You said quarter past five on your postcard.”

“The four-ten train from Lincoln had a tailwind and an impatient driver,” she said. “And I’ve never known a Locke to complain when someone arrived twenty minutes ahead of a bill. Put the kettle on the paraffin stove, there’s a good lad, and tell the van man where to stack those three chests before he charges me an extra sixpence for breathing Wharf Lane air.”

Jude grinned—a quick, crooked flash that made him look twelve again—and went out into the drizzle without a coat.

Miriam walked slowly down the narrow aisle between the cases of pica and long primer. Her brother Thomas had kept the print-shop for thirty-four years, long after every merchant in Gainsborough had gone over to offset lithography and cheap glossy handbills. People had called him a mule to his face and a saint behind his back, though Thomas himself would have made no bones about it: he simply liked the weight of iron that only moved when you pulled the bar with your own shoulder.

On the composing stone beside the north window lay a single sheet of damp rag paper, weighted at the corners with four brass quoins. In the center of the sheet sat the impression of a marsh fritillary—Plate 19 from her unfinished county flora—printed in sepia ink so crisp that every vein of the lower wing stood up like fine thread on linen.

Beneath the engraving, in Thomas’s slanted copperplate pencil, was the old press-house mark he had written on every trial sheet since their father’s time: *[Hold for proof.]*

Miriam touched the corner of the paper with her thumb. The ink was dry, which meant he had pulled it at least four days before the stroke took him.

The shop-bell above the street door gave its flat, tinny rattle—a sound like a spoon dropped into an empty saucepan—and Mr. Aldous Pimm stepped over the sill before Jude had even finished carrying in the second tea-chest.

Mr. Pimm was seventy-three, wore a bowler hat indoors unless there was a bishop present, and owned half the brick warehouses between the bridge and the old timber basin. He carried a brass-ferruled walking stick that he used less for balance than for pointing out damp patches on other people’s plaster.

“Good afternoon to you, Mrs. Locke,” Mr. Pimm said, inclining his head by precisely half an inch. “A dismal day for moving paper. The river’s within eight inches of the flood-mark at Morton Ferry.”

“Miss Locke, Mr. Pimm, as it has been since nineteen-sixty,” Miriam replied pleasantly. “And paper in waxed tea-chests minds the rain a good deal less than your roof-gutters appear to. The front gable is dripping straight onto the pavement.”

Mr. Pimm cleared his throat, a sound like coal shifting in a cellar. “We are having the slate-man look at it in April. No harm in looking, as my late father used to say.” He tapped the tip of his stick against the base of the Albion press. “I came across the lane because young Jude here tells me you intend to clear the premises by Saturday week.”

“We have the lease until the twenty-eighth of the month,” Jude said, setting the third chest down beside the stove with a thud that made the tin mugs on the shelf chatter. His ears had gone red at the tips. “My uncle paid the quarter’s rent in January, Mr. Pimm. The receipt is on the brass spike by the ledger.”

“Hush, Jude,” Miriam said mildly, though she did not take her eyes off the landlord. “Put the water on. Mr. Pimm didn’t cross Wharf Lane in the wet to argue with a brass spike. What is it you’re after, Mr. Pimm?”

The older man rested both gloved hands atop his stick. “The hardware firm from Scunthorpe wants the ground floor for storing agricultural twine. They are prepared to take the lease on Monday at twelve pounds a month—four pounds more than your brother ever paid me—provided this iron monster is out of the back room.” He nodded at the Albion press. “A scrap-iron dealer from Bawtry offered me eighteen shillings to break it up on the spot. Save you the haulage to Norwich, Miss Locke. A machine like that isn’t worth a brass farthing to anyone under eighty.”

For a long five seconds the only sound in the print-shop was the blue flame of the paraffin stove purring under the kettle and the steady drip of rain from the blocked gutter outside.

Jude took half a step forward, his jaw set so hard he looked as if he had swallowed a ruler. “That press was cast by Hopkinson and Cope in eighteen-fifty-two, Mr. Pimm! The platen’s truer than any machine between here and York. If a scrap-man lays a sledgehammer on that staple, I’ll—”

“Jude,” Miriam said. Just the name, spoken in the low, level voice she used when a student at the technical college was about to knock over a jar of nitric acid.

Jude stopped, though his hands remained balled inside the canvas apron.

Miriam turned back to the composing stone. Beside the proof of the marsh fritillary lay a wooden box containing fourteen blocks of end-grain pearwood, each carved by her own hand over seven winters and sent up to Thomas one by one by parcel post. Twelve had been printed and stacked in the drying rack overhead. Two remained uncut in the forme, waiting for the final pull.

“My brother,” Miriam said, picking up one of the brass quoins and turning it in her fingers, “spent thirty-four years keeping his powder dry in this room, Mr. Pimm. He never owed you a Friday’s rent, and when the river came into the lane in sixty-nine, it was Thomas who sandbagged your grain-store door at three in the morning while you were up in Lincoln.”

“Nobody says Thomas Locke wasn’t a painstaking tenant,” Mr. Pimm said, shifting his weight slightly so a floorboard creaked beneath his heel. “Business is business, Miss Locke. You can’t print butterflies for a living when the town wants binder twine. Still—no harm in looking at an arrangement, if the boy wants to clear the timber out by Friday.”

“The press stays until the twenty-eighth,” Miriam said. “And on the twenty-seventh it goes onto a flatbed lorry intact, every bolt and brass washer of it, bound for the printing school in Norwich. If your scrap-man from Bawtry sets one boot inside that doorway before noon on the twenty-eighth, I shall have the town constable down here faster than a scalded cat. Good afternoon, Mr. Pimm.”

Mr. Pimm looked from Miriam’s face to Jude’s clenched fists, weighed his prospects like a grocer eyeing a suspect turnip, and touched the brim of his bowler hat. “The twenty-eighth at noon, then. Not an hour past. Mind the step as you carry those chests; the mortar’s loose.”

The shop-bell rattled once more as the door shut behind him, leaving the smell of wet wool and cigar ash hanging in the cold air.

“You didn’t even raise your voice,” Jude said after a moment, staring at the closed door. “I thought you were going to let him have the press.”

“Raise my voice to Aldous Pimm?” Miriam snorted, pulling off her woollen gloves finger by finger. “You might as well bark at a milestone, Jude. It only encourages the moss. Now stop looking like a thunderstorm in a teacup and bring me that tin of black proofing ink from the third drawer.”

Jude blinked. “We’re not packing the pearwood blocks?”

“We have nineteen plates in this flora, and seventeen of them are in those drying racks,” Miriam said, rolling her coat sleeves up to the elbows with practiced neatness. “Your father and I started this book when you were in short trousers. I am not sending the Albion to Norwich with two blocks left unpulled, and I am certainly not leaving that note of his sitting on the stone as if we gave up by the skin of our teeth.”

She pointed to the margin of the damp rag sheet: *[Hold for proof.]*

Jude’s shoulders dropped an inch, the stiffness going out of him all at once. He reached into the third drawer of the cabinet, brought out the round tin of stiff black ink and the leather-handled brayer, and laid them on the ink-table with a quiet care that belonged entirely to his uncle. “The paper’s still damp enough in the wetting-trough, Aunt Miriam. Uncle Tom damped two quires on Thursday night. Look—he even marked the register pins.”

Together they worked until the March dusk turned the north window the colour of pewter. Jude inked the pearwood blocks with long, even strokes of the roller until the carved lines gleamed like wet slate, while Miriam laid each sheet against the brass pins and folded the frisket down. When Jude threw his weight onto the winter-cold iron bar of the Albion, the toggle joint came home with its familiar, solid thump—not loud, but deep in the floorboards, like a heart deciding to keep beating.

At six o’clock—six slow strokes from the parish church bell across the water—they lifted the final sheet of Plate 19 from the tympan and held it close to the green-shaded paraffin lamp. Every wing-scale, every hair on the marsh grass stem stood clean and black against the cream rag paper.

Miriam took Thomas’s carpenter’s pencil from the tray beside the stone. Beneath his slanted *[Hold for proof.]*, she drew a neat horizontal line and wrote, in a hand so like his that Jude had to look twice:

*[Passed for press. M.L. & J.L.]*

“There,” Miriam said softly, setting the pencil back in its groove. “Now take that kettle off the stove before it boils dry, Jude. We’ve seven drawers of lead type to pack before the nine o’clock train, and I’ve no intention of leaving Mr. Pimm so much as a bent quadrat.”
