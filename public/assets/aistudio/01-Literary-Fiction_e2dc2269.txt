WHAT THIS STORY TESTS
- Subtext: the characters never name their grief. The translation must
  not "explain" what the English leaves unsaid.
- Restraint of register: short, plain sentences with heavy emotional
  load. Over-ornate Bangla would betray the voice.
- Kinship address between a father and an adult daughter (tumi / apni /
  tui choice and its consistency).
- Small domestic objects as emotional carriers (latch, tea, radio).

STORY
----------------------------------------------------------------

THE WINDOW LATCH

Mira got off the afternoon bus at the bend in the road where the tea stall used to be. The stall was gone. In its place stood a shuttered shop with a faded picture of a mobile phone painted on its door. She stood for a while with her bag at her feet, as though the town might notice her and make an announcement.

Her father's house was ten minutes away on foot. She took fifteen.

The gate was open. It had always been open; her mother used to say a closed gate was an invitation to ask who was behind it. The mango tree had dropped its first small fruit in the yard, green and hard, and nobody had swept them up. Mira stepped around them carefully, the way you step around sleeping animals.

"You came," her father said from the veranda.

He did not get up. He was sitting in the cane chair with a newspaper folded into a square on his knee, and he looked, she thought, exactly the same, which was somehow worse than if he had looked different.

"The bus was on time," she said.

"It is never on time."

"It was today."

He nodded slowly, as if this were a piece of news he would need to think about. Then he said, "There is tea."

There was. A steel flask stood on the small table with two cups beside it, both clean, both turned upside down on a saucer. He had set out two. Mira noticed it and did not say so. She sat down on the step instead of the second chair, and he poured, and the tea came out the colour of weak brick, too sweet, exactly as it always had when he made it. Her mother had made it darker.

"The house is quiet," she said.

"The radio is on in the evenings."

"That's not the same."

"No," he agreed. "It is not the same." And he drank his tea.

---

She slept in her old room. The bed had been made with sheets she did not recognise, and the pillow smelled of the sun, so someone had aired it that morning. She lay on top of the blanket and listened to the house. A fan ticked. Somewhere a tap dripped, one patient drop at a time. Outside the window, the latch rattled in the wind: a small, thin, metallic sound, like a fingernail on a tin cup.

It was the latch of the window above the desk. It had never closed properly. For as long as Mira could remember it had hung a little crooked, and on windy nights it had knocked and knocked. Her mother used to say they would fix it in the dry season. Then the dry season would come, and then the rains, and the latch went on knocking, and nobody minded. It was part of the house, like the crack in the third step or the way the kitchen door stuck in August.

She got up and pressed the latch down with her thumb. It slid halfway and stopped. She pushed harder. It stayed where it was, stubborn and slightly bent.

In the morning she would find a screwdriver.

---

"What are you doing?" her father asked.

It was seven o'clock. He stood in the doorway of her room in his vest and lungi, his hair flat on one side from the pillow. Mira was on the desk chair, which she had dragged to the window, holding a screwdriver she had found in the kitchen drawer between the rubber bands and the old keys.

"The latch," she said. "It's loose."

"It has always been loose."

"I know. I thought I would fix it."

He did not say anything for a moment. Mira turned the first screw. It was rusted into its hole and made a small complaining sound.

"You will break it," he said.

"I won't."

"The wood is old. If you turn too hard, the whole piece will split."

"Then I will turn gently."

He came into the room, which he had not done in years, and stood beside her. He smelled of soap and of the oil he put in his hair. He looked at the latch with his head tilted, the way he had once looked at her school reports.

"Your mother wanted it left," he said.

Mira's hand stopped.

"She said she was used to it," he went on. "She said when the house was too quiet she could listen to it and know the wind was still out there doing its work." He cleared his throat. "I thought it was a strange thing to say. I told her I would fix it, and she said, 'Not yet.' So I did not."

The screwdriver was cold in Mira's hand. Outside, a crow shouted at something and was answered.

"I'll stop," she said.

"No."

"Baba—"

"No. Go on." He was looking at the window, not at her. "I only wanted you to know. Go on."

She turned the screw again, very gently, and it gave.

---

They did not talk about it over breakfast, or through the long, bright middle of the day. They talked about other things. He asked if the city was hot, and she said it was, and he said it was a hot year everywhere. She asked about the neighbour's daughter, who had gone to Dubai, and he said she had sent a refrigerator home and a photograph of herself standing beside it. They both laughed at that, a little more than it deserved, and then both stopped at the same moment, as if the laughter had gone somewhere neither of them could follow.

In the afternoon he fell asleep in his chair. Mira took the newspaper from his knee and folded it again, into a neat square, and put it on the table. She stood looking at him for some time. His mouth was slightly open. His hands, which had always seemed so large, rested on the arms of the chair like tools put down at the end of a day.

She went to the kitchen and made tea. She made it dark, the way her mother had: three spoons of leaves, boiled until the water turned the colour of the evening river, a little less sugar. She poured it into the two clean cups and carried them out to the veranda, and sat down in the second chair for the first time.

He woke when the cup touched the table.

"Ah," he said. He looked at the tea. He did not say what he was thinking. He lifted the cup and drank, and Mira watched his face, and he put the cup down and said, "It is stronger."

"Yes."

"It is good."

"I thought so."

They sat. The light moved slowly along the veranda floor and reached the edge of the step. Down at the road, the evening bus went by, and neither of them remarked on whether it was on time.

---

On her last morning she stood in the doorway of her room with her bag. The window above the desk was open, and the latch hung where it had always hung: crooked, slightly loose, free. She had loosened the screws, cleaned the rust from the pin, oiled the hinge with a drop from her father's hair-oil bottle, and then, with a small, private feeling she could not have explained, she had tightened nothing at all.

It would still knock in the wind.

Her father was waiting on the veranda. He had put his good shirt on, which meant he planned to walk her to the bend, though neither of them would say so.

"Did you fix it?" he asked.

"Not yet," Mira said.

He looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind his face, like a curtain in a room where a window has been opened. Then he nodded, once, and picked up her bag before she could stop him.

"The dry season is coming," he said.

"Yes."

"We will see."

They went out through the open gate and past the green fruit lying in the yard. She did not look back at the house. She knew the window was open behind them, and that tonight, if the wind came, the latch would sound against its frame, small and thin and faithful, and her father would lie in the dark and listen to it.

At the bend in the road, where the tea stall used to be, he handed her the bag. He did not hug her. He put his hand on the top of her head, briefly, the way he had when she was seven, and took it away.

"Drink your tea," he said. "While it is hot."

She laughed. It came out unsteady. She said she would.

The bus, for once, was late.

VOICE REFERENCE (for the translator)
----------------------------------------------------------------
- Narration: quiet, observant, understated. Short declarative
  sentences. No melodrama. Feelings are shown by objects and gestures.
- Baba (father): sparse, formal-leaning, speaks in short statements.
  Never says "I miss her" or names the death. Daughter addresses him
  as "Baba". Suggested: father uses "tumi" to Mira; Mira uses "tumi"
  to him (warm but not casual).
- Mira: gentle, dry humour, deflects with small talk.
- The words "death", "died", "funeral" and "grief" never appear in the
  story. Do not introduce them in translation.
